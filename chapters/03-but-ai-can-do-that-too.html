Chapter 03

But AI Can Do That Too

There is an obvious response to almost every argument about why humans will still be needed.

But AI can do that too.

Developers? AI can write code.

QA engineers? AI can generate tests and analyse failures.

DevOps? AI can inspect logs, generate configurations and troubleshoot infrastructure.

Architects? AI can propose system architectures and compare technical choices.

Managers? AI can summarise information, track work and identify risks.

So simply saying "humans are needed because AI cannot do this" is becoming a weaker argument.

We need a better one.

Capability isn't the same as suitability

Imagine asking an extremely capable AI system:

"Design the best architecture for this product."

It might produce an excellent answer.

It might reason about scalability, reliability, security, cost and performance.

It might even produce several alternatives and explain the trade-offs between them.

But there is a problem hidden inside the word best.

Best according to what?

Lowest cost?

Fastest delivery?

Highest reliability?

Lowest operational complexity?

Strongest security?

Easiest migration from an existing system?

Or perhaps the architecture that a small team can realistically maintain for the next five years?

These objectives can conflict with each other.

A highly probable or well-reasoned answer is not automatically the optimal answer for your specific use case.

The missing context

Software does not exist in a vacuum.

A system exists inside a company. A company exists inside a market. And every technical decision is constrained by reality.

There may be an existing codebase that nobody wants to rewrite.

There may be a fixed budget.

There may be a deadline that cannot move.

There may be regulatory requirements.

There may be legacy systems.

There may be a team that has deep expertise in one technology but very little experience with another.

There may be customers who will tolerate a slow interface but will never tolerate losing their data.

Those details change the answer.

What humans may actually contribute

This is where I think the role of the engineer begins to move upward.

The value is less about manually producing every line of code and more about understanding what should be produced in the first place.

Someone has to understand the business problem.

Someone has to understand the constraints.

Someone has to decide which trade-offs are acceptable.

Someone has to determine whether the result is actually correct.

And someone has to own the consequences when it isn't.

But what if AI becomes better at all of this?

This is the harder question.

We cannot assume that humans will always have a permanent advantage in reasoning, planning or contextual understanding.

AI systems may become dramatically better at these things.

If they do, the argument for human involvement cannot simply be: "AI can't do it."

Because eventually, it might.

The more durable question is:

Who is responsible for deciding what outcome we actually want, what risks we are willing to accept, and whether the system should be allowed to act?

That is not purely a technical question.

It is also a question of responsibility, incentives, governance and accountability.

The engineer doesn't disappear

I don't think the future necessarily looks like humans versus AI.

A more realistic possibility is humans working at a different level because AI handles more of the execution.

The engineer becomes someone who can understand a problem deeply, give AI the right direction, evaluate what it produces, correct it when necessary, and take responsibility for the final result.

In that world, knowing how to code still matters.

But knowing only how to code may not be enough.

The question is no longer simply whether AI can do the work.

It is whether we can become good enough at directing, evaluating and owning the work that AI helps us produce.
