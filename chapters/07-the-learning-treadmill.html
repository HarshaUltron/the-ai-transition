Chapter 07

The Learning Treadmill

There is another consequence of the AI transition that is easy to overlook.

Even if AI does not replace you, it may change what it means to remain valuable.

The expectation to keep learning has always existed in technology.

A developer learns a programming language.

Then a framework.

Then cloud.

Then containers.

Then Kubernetes.

Then AI.

Then agents.

Then another model.

And another platform.

The cycle never seems to stop.

The treadmill gets faster

AI may make that cycle even faster.

A technology that was considered advanced a year ago can suddenly become ordinary.

A workflow that required specialised knowledge can become something an AI assistant can perform.

A new model can change how an entire category of software is built.

So engineers are told:

"Keep learning."

And they do.

But there is a point where we need to ask a different question.

How much learning is actually necessary, and how much of it is simply keeping up with the speed of the tools?

The pressure on the individual

Imagine being an engineer working a full day.

You have meetings.

You have production issues.

You have deadlines.

You have client discussions.

You have bugs to fix.

You have a personal life.

And then, after all of that, you are told that you also need to spend several hours every day learning the newest AI tools because otherwise someone else will become more productive than you.

The cycle becomes:

Work → Learn → Work → Learn → Repeat.

And if AI continues accelerating, the treadmill may simply get faster.

But there is another side of this

There is an important question for management here.

As AI becomes more capable, companies will naturally want to use it to increase productivity.

That makes sense.

But the way that productivity is interpreted matters enormously.

Imagine a manager saying:

"We have Claude. We have Copilot. We have AI agents. Why do we need another developer?"

That sounds logical at first.

But it can reflect a misunderstanding of what AI productivity actually means.

If AI helps a developer write code twice as fast, that does not necessarily mean the entire project can now be completed twice as fast.

The developer may still be waiting for requirements.

The client may change the requirement.

Another team may control a dependency.

The database may need to be changed.

Security may need to review the implementation.

The deployment environment may have problems.

The AI-generated code may need verification.

Integration may take longer than implementation.

And someone still has to understand whether the thing being built is actually the right thing to build.

What should management actually ask?

I think the management question should change.

Instead of asking:

"Why do we need another developer when AI can do the work?"

The better question is:

"If AI makes this team more productive, what more should this team be able to accomplish?"

That is a very different mindset.

If a team becomes more productive, perhaps the company should build more.

Perhaps it should take on more customers.

Perhaps it should improve existing products.

Perhaps it should experiment with ideas that previously weren't economically viable.

Perhaps the same team can now solve problems that previously required a much larger team.

AI should create additional capacity.

The question is what the organisation chooses to do with that capacity.

Productivity is not the same as headcount reduction

This distinction matters because productivity and employment are not the same metric.

If one engineer with AI can accomplish the work that previously required two engineers, that is a productivity improvement.

But if the company simply removes the second engineer and expects the first engineer to continue doing the same amount of work, the organisation may have captured the productivity benefit without actually creating anything new.

The result is fewer people doing the same work.

That may improve short-term efficiency.

But it also raises a much bigger question about where the additional human capacity goes.

If AI genuinely makes people dramatically more productive, perhaps the goal should be to expand what those people can accomplish rather than simply reducing the number of people required.

What should managers understand?

Managers operating in an AI-heavy environment will need to understand more than just which AI tools are available.

They need to understand what AI can realistically accelerate.

They need to understand what it cannot accelerate.

They need to distinguish implementation time from total delivery time.

They need to account for requirements, dependencies, validation, security, integration and production risk.

And most importantly, they need to understand that giving someone an AI tool does not automatically give them unlimited capacity.

A developer using AI still needs time to think.

They still need to review the output.

They still need to understand the system.

They still need to make decisions.

And they still need to deal with everything around the code.

The right management question is not:

"How quickly can AI make this person finish the task?"

It is:

"What becomes possible for this team now that AI has removed some of the execution burden?"

The responsibility of engineers

This does not mean engineers can stop learning.

Quite the opposite.

Engineers will need to understand AI deeply enough to use it effectively.

But I don't think the answer is becoming an expert in every new tool.

The durable advantage is understanding fundamentals and developing the ability to learn quickly when the tools change.

You don't need to know every framework.

You need to understand how software works.

You don't need to memorise every AI platform.

You need to understand how to evaluate and use AI systems.

You don't need to chase every new trend.

You need to recognise which changes actually matter.

The treadmill should not become the destination

Continuous learning is valuable.

But learning for the sake of keeping up is not the same as building durable capability.

The goal should not be to become 10/10 at everything.

It may be better to become deeply capable in a few areas while having enough breadth to understand the rest and direct AI effectively.

The engineer of the future may need fewer hours of manual execution and more hours of understanding, deciding, reviewing and directing.

That is a healthier way to think about the transition.

AI should not turn every engineer into someone permanently afraid of becoming obsolete.

It should give engineers more leverage.

And organisations should think carefully about how that leverage is used.

Because if the only definition of productivity is "how many people can we eliminate?", we may be measuring only one side of what AI makes possible.

The more interesting question is:

What could we build if humans spent less time executing and more time thinking?

That question leads to another difficult issue.

Because if AI really does make a small team capable of doing the work of a much larger team, what happens to everyone who is no longer needed?

That is the question we need to confront next.
