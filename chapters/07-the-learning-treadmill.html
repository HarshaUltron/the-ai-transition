Chapter 07

The Learning Treadmill

There is another consequence of the AI transition that is easy to overlook.

Even if AI doesn't eliminate your job, it may change what it takes to remain employable.

And that can create a different kind of pressure.

Work. Learn. Repeat.

For many engineers, the cycle already looks familiar.

Work during the day.

Learn something new after work.

Study a new framework.

Learn a new cloud service.

Understand the latest AI model.

Practice system design.

Solve coding problems.

Prepare for interviews.

Then go back to work and start again.

The technology changes. The learning never seems to stop.

AI makes the treadmill faster

AI may actually accelerate this cycle.

If AI makes engineers more productive, companies may expect more from each engineer.

If tools become easier to use, the baseline expectation may rise.

If one engineer can accomplish more, then being "good enough" today may not mean being good enough five years from now.

The result could be a strange paradox:

Technology makes work easier, but staying competitive may become harder.

The temptation to learn everything

This creates another problem.

AI is moving so quickly that there always seems to be something new to learn.

A new model.

A new agent framework.

A new protocol.

A new cloud service.

A new development tool.

A new benchmark.

A new technique that everyone suddenly says is essential.

Trying to become an expert in all of them is impossible.

What should we actually learn?

Perhaps the answer is not to chase every new technology.

It is to build a strong foundation and learn how to adapt.

Programming fundamentals matter.

Data structures matter.

Databases matter.

Networking matters.

Distributed systems matter.

Security matters.

System design matters.

Understanding how businesses actually work matters.

These concepts don't become useless because a new AI model appears.

Depth over everything

I don't think the goal should be becoming 10/10 at everything.

That isn't realistic.

A better strategy may be to develop deep expertise in one or two areas while building enough breadth to understand the rest of the system.

An engineer might become exceptionally strong at backend systems while understanding cloud, databases, AI and product requirements well enough to work across them.

Another engineer might specialise deeply in security while using AI to handle much of the implementation work.

The point isn't to know everything.

It is to know what matters.

The treadmill has a limit

There is also a human question here.

People have limited time and energy.

We cannot spend every evening learning just to remain employable.

At some point, the question becomes:

How much of our lives should be spent preparing for the next technological change?

There is no simple answer.

But I think there is an important distinction between continuous learning and continuous panic.

Learning should increase our capability.

It shouldn't become an endless attempt to outrun uncertainty.

The durable advantage

The most durable skill may therefore be the ability to learn, reason and adapt rather than memorising a particular technology.

Tools will change.

Models will change.

Frameworks will change.

But the ability to understand a difficult problem and learn what is necessary to solve it remains valuable.

Maybe that is what we should optimise for.

Not knowing everything.

Not chasing every trend.

But becoming the kind of person who can enter an unfamiliar problem, understand it, use whatever tools are available, and produce a reliable outcome.

And for people entering the workforce during this transition, that may be one of the most important skills of all.
