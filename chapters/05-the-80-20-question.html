Chapter 05

The 80/20 Question

Imagine a software team with seven people.

Developers write the application. QA engineers test it. DevOps engineers manage deployments and infrastructure. Architects think about the system. Managers coordinate everything.

Now imagine that AI becomes good enough that three highly capable engineers, working with AI, can accomplish roughly the same amount of work that those seven people previously accomplished.

This is only a thought experiment.

It is not a prediction that teams will actually shrink from seven people to three.

But it forces us to ask an uncomfortable question.

If technology dramatically increases productivity, what happens to the people whose labour is no longer required at the same scale?

The good news

From the company's perspective, this can look fantastic.

A smaller team can potentially deliver more.

Products can be built faster.

Experiments become cheaper.

More ideas can be tested.

Engineers can spend less time on repetitive work and more time on difficult problems.

This is what technological progress has often promised: greater productivity and greater output with less human effort.

But productivity and employment are different questions

Suppose those three engineers really can do the work of seven.

The company may have several choices.

It could keep seven people and use the additional capacity to build more products.

It could reduce the team to five.

It could reduce it to three.

Or it could decide that the extra productivity creates an opportunity to enter entirely new markets.

The technology itself doesn't decide which choice a company makes.

Management decisions, economics, competition and incentives do.

What if every company does it?

This is where the thought experiment becomes much larger.

One company becoming more productive is a competitive advantage.

An entire industry becoming more productive is an economic transformation.

If every software company eventually needs fewer people to produce the same amount of software, the question is no longer just about individual companies.

It becomes a labour-market question.

Where do the displaced workers go?

What new jobs appear?

Are the new jobs accessible to the people whose previous jobs disappeared?

How long does that transition take?

And what happens to people during the transition?

The optimistic argument

There is a strong argument that new technology creates new work.

The internet created entire industries that didn't exist before it.

Smartphones created businesses, jobs and markets that would have been difficult to imagine decades earlier.

AI may do the same.

Perhaps today's software engineers will move into entirely new categories of work.

Perhaps cheaper software will create millions of new businesses.

Perhaps AI will make previously impossible products economically viable.

That is possible.

The uncomfortable counterargument

But there is an important difference between creating new work and creating enough new work for everyone whose old work disappeared.

A new industry may require different skills.

It may require fewer people.

It may emerge in a different country.

Or it may take years to develop.

People still have rent, loans, families and expenses during those years.

That is why the transition matters as much as the eventual outcome.

What should companies do?

There is another way to think about AI productivity.

Instead of asking:

"How many people can we eliminate?"

companies could ask:

"How much more can this team accomplish now?"

If AI gives a seven-person team the capacity of a much larger team, perhaps that capacity should be used to build more, experiment more, serve more customers and create new products.

The answer doesn't have to be simply reducing headcount.

The real 80/20 question

The most important question isn't whether the ratio will literally become 80/20.

The number is just a way of thinking about the direction of change.

What happens when a relatively small number of people can produce what once required a much larger workforce?

If the answer is simply "fewer people are needed," then we need to think seriously about what happens to everyone else.

Because productivity gains are good.

Technological progress is good.

But a society also has to decide how the benefits of that progress are distributed.

And that takes us beyond software engineering.

It takes us to India.
