Chapter 09

We Shouldn't Sleep

There are two easy reactions to AI.

One is panic.

The other is dismissal.

I don't think either is useful.

We shouldn't assume that AI will replace everyone.

But we also shouldn't assume that everything will remain exactly the same.

The real question isn't whether AI is powerful

At this point, it is becoming increasingly difficult to argue that AI is simply another ordinary software tool.

It can write code. It can analyse documents. It can generate tests. It can debug problems. It can reason about architectures. It can interact with tools. It can perform tasks that previously required people to move between multiple systems and make decisions along the way.

The important question is therefore not:

"Can AI do this?"

For an increasing number of tasks, the answer is going to be yes.

The harder questions are:

"How well can it do it?"

"How reliably can it do it?"

"When should we trust it?"

"How much human involvement will still be required?"

"And what happens to the people whose jobs contained those tasks?"

Technology can move faster than society

This is where the discussion becomes bigger than software engineering.

A company adopting AI may be able to increase productivity very quickly.

But workers cannot necessarily reinvent their careers at the same speed.

A developer who spent years becoming good at writing software cannot instantly become an architect, product strategist, AI researcher, or business leader simply because an AI coding assistant became powerful.

And if an entire industry changes at the same time, the consequences extend far beyond individual careers.

Fewer technology workers could eventually affect housing demand, restaurants, transportation, retail, education, recruitment, commercial real estate and many other industries that depend on high-income technology employment.

This is particularly important in countries like India, where the technology-services industry supports a massive ecosystem around it.

The productivity paradox

There is something fascinating about this transition.

If AI allows three engineers to accomplish what previously required seven, that is an extraordinary productivity improvement.

But what happens to the other four engineers?

That is the question we cannot answer simply by saying:

"AI will create new jobs."

It might.

History gives us many examples of technology creating new industries and occupations.

But the transition is not automatically painless.

The new jobs may require different skills. They may appear in different places. They may pay differently. And they may not arrive at the same speed at which the old jobs disappear.

That gap matters.

We need to think beyond individual adaptation

A common response to technological change is:

"Just keep learning."

There is truth in that.

Learning will matter enormously.

But it cannot be the entire answer.

If an entire workforce is told to continuously reskill itself every few years while technology keeps accelerating, we should also ask whether companies, governments, universities and society are doing enough to help people make that transition.

The responsibility cannot sit entirely on the individual.

This is not a prediction

I don't know what percentage of software jobs AI will eventually automate.

I don't know whether it will be 20 percent, 50 percent, 80 percent, or something completely different.

Anyone claiming to know that number with certainty is probably selling confidence rather than knowledge.

The point of this book is not to predict a specific number.

It is to ask what happens if the capability continues improving.

Because if we wait until the outcome is obvious, the opportunity to prepare for it may already have passed.

The Titanic analogy

Sometimes technological transitions remind me of the story of the Titanic.

Not because AI is a ship, and not because the future is predetermined.

But because confidence can become dangerous when it turns into complacency.

A system can appear enormous, successful and stable right up until the assumptions underneath it are challenged.

Software engineering is not going to disappear overnight.

Companies will still need people who understand systems, businesses will still need people who make decisions, and society will still need people who take responsibility for important outcomes.

But the shape of that work can change dramatically.

So we shouldn't sleep

We shouldn't panic.

We shouldn't blindly believe every prediction about mass unemployment either.

But we also shouldn't sleep through the transition.

We should be asking uncomfortable questions now.

What jobs will change?

How many people will those jobs require?

Which skills will become more valuable?

Which skills will become less valuable?

Who will benefit?

Who will struggle to transition?

And what should we do before those answers become obvious?

For engineers, the answer may be to understand fundamentals more deeply, learn how to work with AI, develop better judgment, understand business problems and become capable of owning outcomes rather than simply producing code.

For companies, the question should not only be how many people AI can replace.

It should also be what becomes possible when humans and machines work together.

And for society, the question is even bigger:

What happens when the ability to produce more no longer requires proportionally more people?

I don't know the answer.

But I think we should start discussing it before we are forced to.

Because technological change is rarely just about technology.

It is about people.

It is about careers.

It is about economies.

And ultimately, it is about how we decide to use the capabilities we create.

The future is not written yet.

But it is being built right now.

We shouldn't wait until the change is undeniable before we start understanding its consequences.

One Last Question

There is a scene from I, Robot that captures a similar question beautifully.

"Can a robot write a symphony?
Can a robot turn a canvas into a beautiful painting?"
"Will you?"

I, Robot (2004)

I don't expect everyone to agree with it. In fact, disagreement is probably more interesting.
