Chapter 01

The Question

I first heard about Astra, and something immediately bothered me.

Not because it was another impressive AI product.

But because it made me think about where all of this is actually heading.

If an AI system can understand a problem, reason through it, write the code, test it, debug it, and eventually handle more and more of the work around software development, then what happens to the people whose careers were built around doing those things?

That was the question that started this entire thought process.

If AI can increasingly do the work, what happens to the people who used to do it?

I have spent years learning how to build software.

Learning programming.

Learning backend development.

Learning databases, APIs, cloud infrastructure, system design and everything else that comes with becoming a better engineer.

Like many engineers, I assumed that getting better at these things would make my career safer and more valuable over time.

And traditionally, that assumption made sense.

Technology changed, but the need for people who understood technology remained.

A developer became a senior developer.

A senior developer became an architect or technical lead.

People took on more responsibility, solved harder problems and moved upward.

But AI makes me question whether that ladder will continue to work in exactly the same way.

What happens when execution becomes cheap?

Imagine giving an AI a well-defined problem along with the requirements, constraints and expected outcome.

It can potentially write the implementation.

Another system can test it.

Another can analyse failures.

An agent can interact with cloud infrastructure.

Documentation can be generated automatically.

Architecture can be proposed.

And increasingly, these systems can work together.

The obvious reaction is:

"Then developers will be replaced."

But I don't think the answer is that simple.

Because software engineering has never been only about typing code.

Someone still has to understand what the business actually needs.

Someone has to decide which requirements matter and which don't.

Someone has to understand the consequences of a technical decision.

Someone has to decide whether a solution is secure enough, reliable enough, affordable enough and appropriate for the situation.

And ultimately, someone has to take responsibility when the system fails.

That is where the conversation becomes much more interesting.

The human contribution moves upward

Maybe the future isn't about humans competing with AI at writing code line by line.

Maybe it is about humans moving higher up the stack.

Instead of spending most of our time producing implementation, we may spend more time defining problems, evaluating solutions, making trade-offs, directing AI systems and taking ownership of outcomes.

But even that creates another uncomfortable question.

What if AI becomes good at those things too?

That is the question I want to explore in the chapters that follow.

Not whether AI is good or bad.

Not whether developers will disappear tomorrow.

But what happens to software engineering, careers and the economy if machines continue becoming capable of doing more and more of the work humans currently perform.

And perhaps the biggest question of all:

If the machines become dramatically more productive, what happens to the humans?
