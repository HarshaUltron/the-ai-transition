Chapter 02

The Software Team

To understand what AI might change, it helps to first understand what a software team traditionally looks like.

A software product is rarely built by one person doing everything. Different people take responsibility for different parts of the process.

There is the developer who writes the application.

There is QA, making sure the application actually works and identifying what breaks.

There is DevOps, responsible for infrastructure, deployments, environments, monitoring and reliability.

There are architects thinking about how the system should be structured and how its different components should work together.

And there are managers coordinating people, priorities, timelines, stakeholders and expectations.

None of these roles exists in isolation.

They form a chain.

A requirement becomes a design. A design becomes code. Code becomes a tested application. The application gets deployed. Someone monitors it. Someone decides what should happen next.

For decades, this division of work has been a practical way of building software.

Then AI enters the team

Now imagine giving an AI system the requirements, the existing codebase, the expected behaviour, the constraints and access to the tools required to execute the work.

It can potentially write the implementation.

It can generate tests.

It can inspect logs and diagnose failures.

It can suggest architectural changes.

It can generate deployment configurations.

It can produce documentation.

And increasingly, AI agents can execute multi-step workflows rather than simply responding to individual prompts.

Suddenly the boundaries between these roles start becoming less rigid.

The developer

The most obvious change is to software development itself.

Writing code is becoming dramatically easier.

A developer can describe a feature and have an AI generate much of the implementation.

That does not make engineering irrelevant.

But it can change where the engineer spends their time.

Less time typing every line. More time understanding the problem, reviewing the implementation, debugging unexpected behaviour and deciding what should actually be built.

QA

Testing is also increasingly automatable.

AI can generate test cases, identify edge cases, analyse failures and help reproduce bugs.

That doesn't mean quality becomes automatic.

Someone still needs to determine whether the system behaves correctly in the real world.

The definition of "correct" is often larger than whether a test passed.

DevOps

Infrastructure work is changing too.

AI can generate deployment configurations, analyse logs, suggest fixes and help operate cloud environments.

But production systems have consequences.

A bad deployment isn't just a bad answer in a chat window. It can cause downtime, data loss, security incidents or financial damage.

Architecture

Architecture may look safer because it involves higher-level reasoning.

But AI can already propose architectures, compare technologies and reason about trade-offs.

The interesting question is therefore not whether AI can produce an architecture diagram.

It is whether the proposed architecture is actually appropriate for this particular organisation, this particular product and these particular constraints.

Management

Even management contains tasks that AI can increasingly assist with: summarising meetings, tracking work, analysing progress, writing updates and identifying risks.

But management also involves people, incentives, conflict, accountability and decisions under uncertainty.

Those responsibilities don't disappear simply because information becomes easier to process.

The uncomfortable possibility

The real disruption may therefore not be that one AI replaces one job.

It may be that AI changes the amount of human labour required across the entire software development process.

A team that once required seven people might eventually accomplish a similar amount of work with three highly capable engineers using AI.

The question then becomes bigger than:

Can AI do my job?

It becomes:

How many people will companies need to do the same amount of work?

And that is where the conversation becomes much more complicated.
