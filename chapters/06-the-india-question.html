Chapter 06

The India Question

The impact goes beyond software engineers

Imagine something as ordinary as going to a restaurant.

A bottle or can of Diet Coke might cost around ₹50 in a store. But in a restaurant, the same drink can easily become a ₹200–₹300 purchase, served in a glass with ice.

Why would someone pay that much?

Because for someone earning a relatively high salary, spending ₹300 on a drink occasionally may not feel like a big decision.

The same idea applies to restaurants, cafés, food delivery, gyms, salons, cabs, shopping, apartments and many other services.

A large population of relatively well-paid technology workers creates demand for all of these things.

Now imagine that AI significantly reduces the number of people earning those salaries.

The first question would be:

How many software engineers are affected?

But there is a second question that is much bigger:

Who is going to keep spending the money that supported all the businesses around them?

The restaurant doesn't only lose a technology worker as a customer.

The delivery driver may get fewer orders.

The café may get fewer customers.

The gym may lose memberships.

The salon may see fewer appointments.

The cab driver may get fewer trips.

The landlord may face lower demand for expensive apartments.

And every one of those businesses employs people too.

That is why the AI transition cannot be viewed only through the lens of software engineering.

India's IT economy

This question matters particularly in India.

For decades, India's technology industry has been one of the country's most important sources of high-skilled employment, foreign revenue and middle-class income.

The industry is also connected to a much larger ecosystem.

Technology companies employ engineers, analysts, testers, project managers and many other professionals.

But around those companies exists another economy.

People rent apartments because they work in technology.

Restaurants open near technology parks because thousands of employees need lunch.

Cafés exist because people work from them or meet colleagues there.

Transport services grow because employees commute.

Retail businesses benefit from disposable income.

Real estate markets respond to where these workers live.

Even local construction and service businesses can benefit indirectly from the concentration of relatively high-income workers.

This creates a multiplier effect.

The money earned by one industry does not stay inside that industry.

It gets spent elsewhere.

What happens when the spending slows?

Suppose AI makes software development dramatically more productive.

Companies might be able to build the same amount of software with smaller teams.

That could be excellent for productivity.

It could mean faster products, lower costs and more innovation.

But if fewer people receive the salaries that previously supported that spending, another part of the economy could feel the effect.

This doesn't necessarily mean that the economy collapses.

New industries could emerge.

New jobs could be created.

People could move into entirely different types of work.

And AI could create new forms of wealth that we cannot currently predict.

But the transition matters.

Because the people losing income today cannot necessarily wait for an industry that might exist five or ten years from now.

The concentration problem

There is another issue.

Technology jobs are not distributed evenly across the country.

They are concentrated in particular cities and economic centres.

When a large number of high-income workers are concentrated in one area, entire local economies grow around them.

That creates prosperity when the industry grows.

But it can also create vulnerability when the industry contracts.

A change in technology can therefore become a change in geography.

The question is not only what happens inside a software company.

It is what happens to the ecosystem surrounding that company.

India's opportunity

There is also a more optimistic way to look at this.

India has a large technology workforce and a huge pool of technical talent.

If AI becomes a productivity multiplier rather than simply a replacement mechanism, India could potentially use that capability to build more products, create new companies and serve entirely new markets.

The same engineers who currently spend their time implementing software could potentially spend more time solving higher-value problems.

Companies could become smaller but more productive.

New businesses could emerge that would have been too expensive to build before.

The economic opportunity could be enormous.

But opportunity and disruption can happen at the same time.

That is the difficult part.

The question we should be asking

Instead of asking only:

"Will AI replace Indian software engineers?"

Perhaps we should ask:

"What happens to an economy when one of its largest high-income employment engines becomes dramatically more productive?"

How many people will still be needed?

What happens to salaries?

What happens to entry-level jobs?

What happens to the cities that grew around technology?

What happens to the businesses that depend on technology workers spending their income?

And most importantly:

Can the economy create enough new opportunities quickly enough for the people affected by the transition?

I don't know the answer.

But I think these questions deserve to be discussed before the answers are forced upon us.
