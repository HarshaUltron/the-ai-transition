Chapter 08

I'm 25

There is a point where a technological change stops feeling like something happening in the news and starts feeling personal.

For me, that point is now.

I'm 25.

In five years, I'll be 30.

That is not particularly old. It is not particularly young either. It is simply an age where the decisions you make about your career begin to feel more consequential.

You start thinking less about your next job and more about whether the skills you are building today will still matter ten years from now.

And that is where AI makes the question uncomfortable.

What if the ladder changes?

Most careers have some version of a ladder.

You learn the fundamentals. You become better at your job. You take on more responsibility. You become senior. Eventually, you may become a lead, architect, manager, or specialist.

The assumption underneath that ladder is that the work will continue to exist.

The tools may change. The technologies may change. But the underlying need for people to perform the work remains.

AI challenges that assumption.

If machines become increasingly capable of writing code, testing software, deploying systems, analysing data, creating documentation, and even making architectural recommendations, then the traditional path from junior engineer to senior engineer may not look the same.

The question isn't simply:

"Will AI replace developers?"

The more important question is:

"What happens to the career path that used to turn developers into senior engineers?"

The uncomfortable middle

There is another possibility that worries me more than complete replacement.

The possibility that AI makes experienced engineers dramatically more productive while simultaneously reducing the number of people companies need to perform the same amount of work.

That creates an uncomfortable middle ground.

You may still need engineers.

You may simply need fewer of them.

And when fewer people are required to produce the same output, competition for those positions naturally increases.

That doesn't mean there will be no jobs.

It means the definition of a valuable engineer may change.

So what should someone my age do?

I don't think the answer is to panic and try to learn every new AI tool that appears.

There will always be another framework, another model, another agent platform, another API, another workflow.

Chasing all of them would be another version of the learning treadmill described earlier in this book.

Instead, I think the goal should be to become difficult to replace because you understand the problem, not because you can type code faster than someone else.

Understand databases.

Understand distributed systems.

Understand networking.

Understand APIs, security, cloud infrastructure and software architecture.

Understand how businesses actually work.

And learn how to use AI extremely well.

Not as a substitute for understanding, but as a multiplier for it.

The question I keep coming back to

I don't know what software engineering will look like when I'm 30.

I don't know how many engineers companies will need.

I don't know whether today's career ladders will still exist in their current form.

And I don't think anyone can honestly answer those questions with certainty.

But uncertainty is not a reason to ignore the change.

It is a reason to understand it better.

Maybe AI creates an incredible period of productivity and new opportunities.

Maybe it causes significant disruption to employment.

Maybe the outcome is somewhere in between.

Probably, the future will contain elements of all three.

What I do know is that the next five years are going to be very different from the last five.

And for someone who is 25 today, that is not an abstract statement about the future.

It is my career.

It is our careers.

And that is why I think we should start asking these questions now.
