Chapter 04

The New Engineer

If AI increasingly handles more of the execution, then the engineer of the future cannot simply be an engineer who writes code faster.

The role has to become broader.

Not necessarily because coding becomes unimportant.

But because the value of manually producing every line of code may decrease as machines become better at producing it.

The engineer may have to move from being primarily an implementer to becoming someone who understands the entire problem and can use AI to solve it.

What still matters?

The fundamentals.

Programming.

Data structures and algorithms.

Databases.

Networking.

Operating systems.

Distributed systems.

APIs.

Security.

Cloud infrastructure.

System design.

These things may actually become more important, not less.

Because if AI writes the implementation for you, you still need to understand whether the implementation makes sense.

You need to know when something is wrong.

You need to understand the trade-offs.

You need to be able to debug what the AI produced.

And you need enough technical depth to know when you should trust the answer and when you shouldn't.

AI becomes part of the engineering workflow

The engineer will also need to become extremely comfortable working with AI.

Not just asking a chatbot to write a function.

The workflow could involve multiple AI systems and agents working together.

One agent might analyse requirements.

Another might explore the existing codebase.

Another might implement the change.

Another might generate and execute tests.

Another might investigate failures.

The engineer becomes the person coordinating and evaluating this process.

That requires a different skill set.

You need to know how to provide context.

How to break down problems.

How to verify results.

How to recognise hallucinations and incorrect assumptions.

How to give precise instructions.

And how to decide when the AI should stop and a human should take over.

From Writing to Directing

This is perhaps the biggest shift I see coming.

For years, a large part of software engineering has been about writing.

Writing functions.

Writing APIs.

Writing queries.

Writing tests.

Writing infrastructure code.

Writing documentation.

But if machines become extremely good at producing those things, the engineer's role gradually moves upward.

The engineer moves from writing the work to directing the work.

Instead of:

"Give me the requirements. I'll write the code."

The emerging model could be:

"Give me the problem. I'll determine what needs to be built, direct AI to build it, evaluate the result, and own the outcome."

That is a very different kind of engineering.

The engineer is no longer measured only by how much code they personally produce.

They may be measured by how effectively they can turn a real-world problem into a correct, reliable and useful system.

And that requires much more than coding.

Understanding the business becomes technical skill

Imagine two engineers using exactly the same AI system.

Both have access to the same models.

Both can generate code at roughly the same speed.

Both can ask the AI to design an architecture.

Yet one engineer may produce a much better solution.

Why?

Because they understand the problem better.

They understand why the customer needs the system.

They understand which requirements are actually important.

They understand the constraints.

They understand what the business can afford.

They understand what risks are acceptable.

And they understand what success actually means.

AI can help execute a solution.

But the quality of the solution still depends heavily on the quality of the problem being understood.

The engineer as a decision-maker

This means engineering may become less about producing answers and more about making decisions.

Which architecture?

Which database?

Which model?

Which trade-off?

Which risks are acceptable?

Which parts should be automated?

Which parts require human review?

When should we ship?

When should we stop and redesign?

Those decisions can have enormous consequences.

And that is why technical fundamentals still matter.

You cannot effectively direct something you don't understand.

But there is another problem

If one engineer with AI can accomplish the work that previously required several engineers, then becoming a better engineer is only one side of the story.

The other side is what happens to the people who are no longer required to perform that work.

If productivity increases dramatically, companies may build more software.

But they may also need fewer people to build the same amount of software.

That takes us beyond the question of what an engineer should learn.

It takes us into a much larger question about productivity, employment and the economy.

And that is where the next chapter begins.
