Chapter 04

The New Engineer

If AI can write more of the code, test more of the software and automate more of the execution, then what exactly should an engineer become?

I don't think the answer is to stop learning how to code.

In fact, I think the opposite may be true.

The fundamentals may become more important precisely because AI makes implementation easier.

Knowing code is still important

If an AI generates a thousand lines of Python, someone still needs to understand whether those thousand lines make sense.

If an AI proposes a database design, someone needs to understand indexing, transactions, isolation, consistency and failure modes.

If an AI proposes a distributed architecture, someone needs to understand networking, concurrency, caching, queues and reliability.

You cannot reliably evaluate something you fundamentally don't understand.

AI may reduce the amount of code an engineer writes manually. It does not necessarily reduce the amount of engineering knowledge required to judge that code.

From writing to directing

The engineer's workflow may increasingly look something like this:

Understand the problem.

Break the problem into smaller pieces.

Give the AI the right context.

Let it execute.

Review the result.

Test it.

Challenge its assumptions.

Correct it.

And finally, own the outcome.

That is a different skill from simply typing code.

But this requires breadth

The modern engineer may need to understand more areas than before.

Backend development.

Databases.

Distributed systems.

Cloud infrastructure.

Security.

AI systems.

System design.

And increasingly, the business domain itself.

This doesn't mean becoming an expert in everything.

It means developing enough understanding across the system to know what matters and where the risks are.

Depth still matters

There is also a danger in reacting to AI by trying to learn every new technology that appears.

Every few months there is another framework, another model, another agent platform, another protocol and another tool.

Chasing all of them can become exhausting.

Durable knowledge is different.

Algorithms don't disappear because a new framework appears.

Databases don't stop having consistency and concurrency problems.

Distributed systems don't stop failing in interesting ways.

Networks still have latency.

Systems still have bottlenecks.

Businesses still have constraints.

Those fundamentals survive technological cycles.

The engineer as an AI multiplier

Perhaps the most useful way to think about the future engineer is not as someone competing with AI, but as someone who multiplies their own capabilities through it.

A strong engineer with AI can potentially investigate problems faster, explore more alternatives, prototype more quickly and automate repetitive work.

But the quality of that multiplication depends heavily on the engineer.

A person who doesn't understand the problem can use AI to produce more wrong answers faster.

A person who understands the problem can use the same system to move dramatically faster toward a good solution.

AI may increase the value of engineering judgment precisely because it makes execution cheaper.

The uncomfortable part

This transition may still reduce the number of engineers companies need for certain kinds of work.

Becoming a better engineer does not guarantee that the market will need exactly the same number of engineers.

That is an important distinction.

Individual capability and total employment are not the same thing.

If one engineer can accomplish the work that previously required three, that is excellent productivity for the engineer and the company.

But it creates a difficult question for the other two people.

And that question leads us to the next chapter.
